Who looks up? Amateur astronomy

Five public archives on who owns the equipment, who files the observations, what draws people outside, and how technology changed the hobby.

Amateur astronomers do more than look. They estimate comet brightness, run automated meteor cameras and count stars from their back gardens, and those records feed real research. This dashboard follows five public archives from 2021 to 2025 to ask three questions: who these amateurs are, what they observe with, and what gets them outside.

01

Where the telescopes are

Ownership is concentrated. The Minor Planet Center lists 2,695 observing sites with usable coordinates, professional and amateur alike, and four countries (the United States, Spain, Italy and Germany) hold 51% of them. The equipment used to file comet observations clusters at a few standard sizes, and the countries with the most sites also have the most active comet observers. That raises the next question: does owning the gear mean using it?

Aperture by instrument type, comet observations

Newtonian reflectors carry the record (23,045 of 47,037 observations, 49%), with most between 15 and 50 cm. Binoculars and refractors sit near 7 and 10 cm. The sharp spikes sit mostly on round metric values rather than inch sizes: 5,092 observations give 30.0 cm against 507 at 30.5 cm (12 inches), and 40 and 50 cm show the same pattern, which suggests observers round their aperture. Only 20.3 cm (8 inches, 1,651 observations) matches an inch size. Each ridge is scaled to its own peak, so compare shapes, not heights.

02

Observation records over time

Contribution is unequal. Owning a telescope and filing observations are different things: COBS received 47,037 comet observations from 265 observers between 2021 and 2025, and a small core supplied most of them. What that core watched shifted from month to month with whichever comet was bright, which sets up the question of what makes people go out at all.

The curve stays near the floor until the last tenth: the 26 most active observers, 10% of the total, filed 74% of all observations.

03

Celestial events compared

Events drive attention, but not every event. Lining comet-observing activity up against 16 eclipses and comet close approaches shows which ones actually pulled observers outside. Meteor showers, recorded by automated cameras rather than by people deciding to go out, give the contrasting case: activity that arrives on schedule every year.

Comet observations per day, one square per day, with event windows outlined

Each square is one day; darker means more observations. The orange-outlined weeks around C/2022 E3 (ZTF) in early 2023 and Tsuchinshan-ATLAS in October 2024 average 42 and 46 observations a day, against 26 across the whole period, while the grey-outlined eclipse weeks look like any other weeks. The busiest stretch, October to December 2025 (53 a day), has no event in the list. It came from several comets at once: C/2025 A6 (Lemmon) made up 43% of October's observations, but in November and December no comet passed 15%, with C/2025 K1 (ATLAS), C/2025 R2 (SWAN) and 3I/ATLAS among the most observed.

Shower meteors by solar longitude, one ring per year

The Perseids (140°), Geminids (261–262°) and Quadrantids (282–283°) peak within one degree of solar longitude every year from 2021 to 2025. Shower meteors rose every year, from 66,132 in 2021 to 259,440 in 2025, as the network added cameras (Section 5), though single peaks did not always rise: the 2024 Geminids ring sits inside 2023's. 04

Community participation

Community sustains it. Not everyone owns a telescope: Globe at Night asks anyone to count the stars they can see, and NASA's Night Sky Network lists the clubs that run public observing nights. Both reach past the equipped core of Sections 1 and 2, into countries and towns with few registered sites.

Globe at Night submissions by country and year

The United States leads every year, but Croatia and Uruguay come next, and neither is a major site holder in Section 1 (5 and 4 sites). Participation is fragile: Uruguay fell from 5,020 submissions in 2021 to 370 in 2024, and total submissions halved from 25,259 to 12,524 over the window.

05

Technology and accessibility

Technology changed access. The Global Meteor Network runs on low-cost video cameras, each connected to a Raspberry Pi and hosted by amateur astronomers and professionals alike (Vida et al., 2021; Western News, 2021), and between 2021 and 2025 the number of active stations rose from 390 to 1,856. If the archive grew because the sky got busier, each camera would record more every year; if it grew because more people joined, the rate per camera would hold steady.

Camera stations vs meteor trajectories, joined in year order

Each year moves up and to the right along the dashed constant-rate line. Trajectories per station stayed between 420 and 556, so the near-fivefold growth in records comes from more cameras, not more meteors. Station codes span 23 country prefixes in 2021 and 40 in 2025.

What it adds up to

Taken together, the five sections describe a hobby that is concentrated but widening. Equipment and observation records sit with a few countries and a few dedicated people. Bright comets, not eclipses, are what bring comet observers out in numbers, and casual programmes and clubs reach places the equipped core does not. The steepest growth came from automated cameras, and comet observers are moving the same way: the share of COBS observations made with a camera rather than by eye rose from 62% in 2021 to 78% in 2025.
